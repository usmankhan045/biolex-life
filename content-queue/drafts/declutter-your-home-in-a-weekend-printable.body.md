You keep telling yourself you'll deal with the clutter "when you have time" — and the time never comes. Meanwhile the counter has become a landing strip for mail and receipts, the closet floor disappeared under shoes and totes months ago, and there's a chair in the bedroom that exists only to hold clothes you're not sure are clean. It's not that you don't care. It's that clutter has crept into every room at once, so wherever you start feels equally hopeless, and a "someday" whole-house overhaul sounds so exhausting you'd rather close the door and scroll instead. The pile-up isn't a character flaw or a lack of willpower. It's the predictable result of a home with no built-in system for deciding what stays and what goes. The good news is that a single focused weekend, broken into small moves, is genuinely enough to turn it around.

## What are the best decluttering hacks for a whole weekend?

The best decluttering hacks all share one principle: shrink every decision until it's too small to dread. Instead of "declutter the house," you work one drawer, one shelf, or one 20-minute zone at a time, and you touch each item only once — keep, donate, or toss, no "maybe" pile. A few proven moves make a weekend fly: set a timer so each zone has a finish line, carry three labeled bags or bins with you room to room, and start with easy wins like expired pantry items or worn-out towels to build momentum before you hit sentimental stuff. Pull everything out of a space so you see the true volume, then only put back what earns its spot. The magic isn't working harder — it's removing the decision fatigue that stalls most people by lunchtime. Small, timed, single-touch decisions are what let you clear a whole home in two days.

## What's the fastest way to declutter a room without getting overwhelmed?

The fastest way to declutter a room is to work in one direction around it and finish each surface before moving on, so you always see visible progress. Overwhelm comes from bouncing between spots and half-finishing five things at once. Instead, pick a corner and move clockwise: clear the flat surfaces first, since a cleared counter or nightstand delivers an instant, motivating win, then work into drawers and closets. Keep a laundry basket for "belongs in another room" items so you never leave mid-task to put something away — you deal with the basket at the end. Set a 20-minute timer per zone; when it rings, you either wrap up or reset it once, which prevents the perfectionism spiral that turns one shelf into a three-hour excavation. If a decision takes more than a few seconds, it's usually a no. Speed here isn't carelessness — it's momentum, and momentum is what carries you to the finish.

## How do you decide what to keep, donate, or toss?

Sort every item into one of three destinations the moment you pick it up — keep, donate, or toss — and never set it back down "to decide later," because later is how clutter rebuilds itself. **Keep** is for things you've genuinely used in the last year and have a home for. **Donate** covers anything usable that you've outgrown, duplicated, or simply don't love — clothes that don't fit, gadgets you replaced, décor that no longer suits you. **Toss** (or recycle) is for the broken, expired, stained, and missing-a-piece. When you stall on an item, ask three quick questions: Have I used this in a year? Would I buy it again today? Do I have a specific place to put it? Two nos means it goes. Bag donations immediately and put them in the car so they actually leave — a donation pile that lingers by the door is just clutter with better intentions. Decisive sorting is the engine of the whole weekend.

## What do you do with everything you're keeping?

Once you've cleared the "no" pile, give every keeper a defined home so the room stays clear past Monday. Group like with like — all batteries together, all medicines together, all the kids' art supplies in one spot — then contain each group so it can't creep back across the counter. This is the one place a small purchase earns its keep: a set of clear stackable bins and a simple label maker turn a tidy shelf into one that stays tidy, because everyone in the house can see where things live and, more importantly, where they return. Store the things you use most at eye level and the rare stuff up high or down low. Leave a little breathing room in every drawer and bin; a space packed to 100% has nowhere to absorb tomorrow's incoming mail and gear. ## How do you keep your home decluttered after the weekend?

Protect your weekend's work with a few tiny daily habits, because a cleared home stays clear only if less comes in than goes out. Adopt a loose one-in-one-out rule: when a new shirt, mug, or toy arrives, one leaves. Do a five-minute nightly reset — walk each main room and return stray items to the homes you just created. Keep a donate box in the closet year-round so offloading is a two-second drop, not a future project. And put a recurring 20-minute "clutter sweep" on the calendar every couple of weeks to catch drift before it snowballs.
