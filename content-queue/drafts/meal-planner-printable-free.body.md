Fill out a weekly meal planner in one short sitting, working from what you already have toward what you still need to buy. ## What should you plan for breakfast, lunch, dinner, and snacks?

Plan each meal to match how much attention it realistically gets, and don't try to make every slot exciting. Breakfast should run on repeat — pick two or three rotating options like oatmeal, scrambled eggs, or yogurt and fruit, and stop reinventing it every morning. Lunches are best batched: cook once and portion into containers, or plan deliberate leftovers from the night before so nobody's scrambling at noon. Dinner is where your planning energy belongs, since it's the meal most likely to collapse into last-minute takeout when you're unprepared. Snacks deserve a line too, because unplanned snacking is where grocery budgets quietly leak — jot a couple of easy options like fruit, cheese, or crackers so there's an answer besides "whatever's in the cabinet." Writing all four into your weekly meal planner turns vague intentions into a shopping list you can act on, and it keeps you from over-buying for meals that never actually needed a plan in the first place.

## How do you stick to meal planning when the week gets busy?

Stick to meal planning by making it a five-minute habit you attach to something you already do, not another chore to remember. Keep the friction low: slip your printed planner into a reusable dry-erase pocket sleeve and hang it on the fridge, so you can wipe it clean and rewrite each week instead of hunting for a fresh copy.
